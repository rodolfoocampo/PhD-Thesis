\chapter[Conclusion]{Conclusion and Design Principles}\label{c:conclusion}

At the beginning of this thesis, I established the following \textbf{core aim}:

\begin{quote}
\emph{How can we design generative AI systems that act as effective co-creators, maintaining human agency while effectively leveraging the creative potential of this technology?}
\end{quote}

I then defined the following \textbf{research questions}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} What are the elements of effective co-creative dialogue between humans and AI?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How do these elements influence user roles and agency in human-AI co-creativity?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} What design principles can guide the development of effective, human-centred co-creative AI systems?
\end{enumerate}

In Chapter 3, I addressed research question one by developing a framework for dialogic co-creativity, consisting of a typology of actions and elements of effective co-creative dialogue. This framework provides the analytical lens for examining Dialogic Co-Creative AI systems throughout the thesis. 

Using that framework as a guiding lens, I examined the subsequent chapters. Each one represented a different type of co-creative AI system.

\section{Further analysis: relationship between dialogic affordances, roles and type of interaction}

Let us look more closely at the elements of dialogic co-creativity present in each co-creative system and their relationship to roles. 

\subsection{Chapter 4: two types of writing AI co-creators}

Let us begin by considering the comparative user studies performed in Chapter 4. Two types of co-creative writing systems were compared in user studies in a creative writing task, one offered interaction with the AI only through a chat interface while the other through a chat and a shared text editor between the human and the AI. 

From the perspective of the DCAI framework, the chat-only interface primarily offered interactions \textit{about} the artefact. The user and the AI could discuss goals, feedback and clarifications through the chat window. However, there was no space for both of them to collaborate together on the writing. On the other hand, the chat-and-editor was developed to enable this shared space of interaction allowing for interaction and communication about the writing, but also interaction directly \textit{through} the writing. 

We can further classify these types of co-creative system based on their level of dialogue shown in Figure \ref{fig:levelsofdialogue}. The interaction between the human and AI in the chat-only interface stayed at the level of language, while in the chat-and-editor happened both in the language space and the artefact space. 

As such, we can understand the chat-only co-creative system offering something akin to \textbf{weak-asymmetric dialogue}, while the chat-and-editor system offered something closer, but not fully, akin to \textbf{full dialogue}. In terms of the principles of dialogic co-creativity, the interaction with the \textbf{chat-only system} saw only the presence of \textbf{bidirectional communication}, while the interaction with the \textbf{chat-and-editor prototype} exhibited both \textbf{bidirectional communication and a shared space}, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:comparativetable}. However, as the reader may note in the same figure, \textbf{mutual influence} and \textbf{mutual understanding} were marked as present but to a limited degree, indicated with a tilde (\string~). The reason for this is that the system was not prompted to actively engage in both influencing the process and modelling the user. In other words, the system was passive rather than active. One could imagine, however, that a co-creative AI system (powered by an LLM or else) could be prompted to engage in this type of behaviour. The AI itself could assume a more active role, leading the process, actively making suggestions, or guiding the user through a scaffolded creative process. It could also be prompted to ask clarifying questions more actively in order to form an accurate model of the user's preferences. Future research could examine how to enable this type of active co-creative behaviour through prompting and conversational design while balancing it with user creative agency.

\subsubsection{How did this influence roles?}

Users in the chat-only interface adopted dynamics closer to a client-producer relationship, with the human user assuming roles such as \textbf{director}, \textbf{requester}, \textbf{outsourcer} and \textbf{ideator}, automating most of the writing process. Users in the \textbf{Chat-and-Editor} reported being more involved and contributing more closely to the writing. While they still reported asking the AI to do the heavy-lifting of writing, they \textbf{reported that ``the writing contained their own words'' more}, and \textbf{described themselves as more involved in the writing process}. 

Consider the following quotes from users of the \textbf{Chat-only interface}:

\begin{quote}
``Vorges wrote 90\% of the story based on my prompts. I just tweaked it a bit.'' (Participant, Chat-only)

``It did everything—I just gave it the topic.'' (Participant 6, Chat-only)

``I gave it the idea, and it just took it from there, writing almost everything.'' (Participant 7, Chat-only)

``I gave it the skeleton of the story, and Vorges fleshed it out—almost like giving the recipe and having it cook the dish.'' (Participant 11, Chat-only)
\end{quote}

On the other hand, consider the following quotes from participants using the \textbf{Chat-and-Editor interface}: 

\begin{quote}
``Vorges gave me ideas, and I picked the ones that worked for me, blending them into my own writing.'' (Participant 1, Chat-and-Editor)

``I wrote the draft, and Vorges made the language more advanced and descriptive.'' (Participant 1, Chat-and-Editor)

``It was like I was the curator of the story—I picked the pieces I liked and left the rest.'' (Participant 8, Chat-and-Editor)

``It was like a brainstorming partner, throwing out ideas I hadn’t thought of.'' (Participant 11, Chat-and-Editor)
\end{quote}


These qualitative findings align with the quantitative results shown in Figure \ref{fig:graphlikert}, where users in the Chat-Only interface were more likely to agree with the statement: ``Vorges (the AI) did most of the work'' than users of the Chat-and-Editor. Conversely, users in the Chat-and-Editor interface were more likely to agree that the final story contained words that they wrote. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{likertmarked.png}
    \caption{Comparison of mean scores for the Likert Scale from Chapter 4, highlighting differences about the level of user involvement in the final work in chat-only vs chat-and-editor prototype groups.}
    \label{fig:graphlikert}

It is important to note, however, that while there were differences in level of agreement, \textbf{both groups still reported agreement or strong agreement about the AI doing most of the work}. Moreover, in both cases, the roles described are those of higher level orchestration: providing ideas, curation, direction, while the AI did the work of putting words on the page. These findings align with similar results reported in the literature: in a systematic review of emerging roles and workflows in human-AI in creative practice, \citet{Palani2024-on} found that users increasingly assume roles at the ``Project'' level while AI assumes roles at the ``Artefact'' level. My own findings suggest that this dynamic is more pronounced in co-creative systems that offer weak-asymmetric dialogue, which allows interaction at the language level, but not at the artefact level through a space for collaboration. This finding is not surprising: systems that do not provide affordances for the user to interact at the artefact level see them engage in fewer artefact-level roles. 

\subsubsection{User Motivations}

Another possible reason for both groups reporting that the AI did most of the writing may lie in their motivations. In my studies, the users were asked to complete a writing task and were incentivised to do so, providing an extrinsic motivation, which has been shown to produce less involvement in the process compared to intrinsic motivation \citep[15]{Amabile1983-lj}. 

\citeauthor{Compton2015-sy}'s work on Casual Creativity serves as a useful theoretical lens to further explain this \citep{Compton2015-sy}. \citeauthor{Compton2015-sy} argues that a crucial yet often overlooked dimension to understand users is their motivations. She argues that there are at least two types of users based on their motivations. On one hand, there are users that are task-oriented and simply want to get the job done. On the other, she proposed the term Casual Creators for an emerging type of user of creative tools. These are users that are process-oriented and engage with the tool for the sake of enjoyment. She argues these two types of users elicit two different types of design spaces: they have different needs and motivations. I will henceforth refer to the former as \textbf{Transactional Creatives}, and the latter as \textbf{Casual Creatives}. 

Given the incentivised nature of my studies, and the assigned creative task, most of the users in the studies in Chapter 4 fall in the \textbf{Transactional Creatives category}, which may explain why they reported\textbf{ that AI did most of the work}. In contrast, a small minority reported engaging in the experience with the intention of playing with the tool, and reported they generally enjoy creative writing. This small minority of users may be understood more as casual creatives. Interestingly, while not sufficient to make a statistically significant claim, these users happened to strongly disagree with the statement ``Vorges (the AI) did most of the work''. 

So while the type of dialogic interaction afforded influenced the role assumed by the users, another crucial aspect, which I did not anticipate at the onset of the research and the study design, are \textbf{user motivations}. Transactional creatives may be inclined to be less involved, assuming outsourcer and directive roles, while casual creatives, who in this case enjoy creative writing may be more inclined to be more involved and assumed roles closer to a creative partnership. One may speculate that casual creatives' experience may benefit from affording full dialogue, having the opportunity to contribute to the artefact level through a shared collaborative space, while transactional creatives may be content with weak-asymmetric dialogue interacting at the level of language, that affords task outsourcing rather than deep involvement. This hypothesis and its potential nuances present a promising area to explore further. 

\subsection{Chapter 5: further analysis of an image-generation co-creative AI professional case study}

In Chapter 5, I described a generative AI image system used in the production of visuals for a nationally printed magazine and newspaper in Australia in collaboration with their creative team. The intention was to produce photographs for the people they have listed that year as the most powerful in Australia, placing them in impossible situations using AI, to showcase their personality and work in creative ways.

The system used exhibited a prompt-generation functionality. There was no conversational interface. In this system, the user simply types a prompt, and an image is generated. Iteration and refinement of the produced artefact is extremely limited. We could not take an image we liked and asked to make targeted changes. Instead, every image was always regenerated from scratch, limiting refinement. The system offered no interface to make manual changes to the image, and to make modifications at the pixel level, we needed to bring the image into a separate design software.

As such, the system simply afforded limited actions about the artefact (Request) to the user and virtually no actions \textit{through} the creation. 

\subsubsection{How did this influence roles?}

With such a linear prompt and response interaction, it may be assumed that the level of dialogue afforded was that of \textbf{pseudo-dialogue}, one where only the user is modelling the system, but the system is not modelling or significantly being influenced by the user interaction. However, interestingly, a crucial feature of the system was that it allowed us to train on our own dataset of images. In our case, we wanted to train on the resemblance of the people we were generating and on our desired style.  This can be understood as an affordance not at the dialogic level, but at a meta dialogic level. The system did not model the user's preferences from repeated interaction, instead, by training the system, we could help the system, if implicitly, model our preferences, and similarly, it allowed us to gain a better understanding of the system, contributing at least partly to the principles of mutual influence and mutual understanding. 

In the previous case study, I discussed how linear interaction patterns, request-generation dynamics may contribute to the user assuming less involved roles where they simply outsource creative production. This may have been the case in the image generation case study in chapter 5, however, by providing us with the capability of training the system, our role became that of curators. We first gathered an initial set of images to train on, we then generated using the system trained on these images, and based on the outputs, went on to collect a different set of images to retrain the system, as well as select some outputs we liked to feedback in the training process. Similarly, throughout the process, we learned to prompt better, understanding what words were mapped to what outputs. Our role, therefore, was that of prompters and curators. We not only curated images to train on, but we produced thousands of images and curated the best ones in a highly involved process that lasted around one month. 

Therefore, it appears that when a system affords the ability to train it with datasets, the user assumes the role of curator both of inputs and outputs. 

\subsubsection{User Motivations}

What can we say about our motivations? Were we acting as Transactional Creatives or as Casual Creatives? The work was done as part of a commercial creativity context, producing visuals for a magazine. However, the motivation of the work itself was not to speed up a process, but rather, the stated intention was to probe the limits of generative AI, and to produce a new type of image that was not possible with traditional photography. Our motivation therefore, was not necessarily to enjoy the process, as a casual creative would, or get the job done quickly, as a transactional creative would, but to probe and push creative boundaries. I propose this type of user can be called Transformational Creative.  A crucial feature for this type of user is, therefore, the ability to meta-interact with the system, being able to mould it, personalise it, train it and even break it. Transforming the tool and their associated possibilities is part of the creative process itself for this type of user. As \citet{Boden2003-hk} describes, transformational creativity happens when the space of possibility is transformed, and this may happen when a new tool is created or an existing one modified. In contrast, a Casual Creative may be more interested simply in traversing an existing space (what Boden calls exploratory creativity), while a Transactional Creative may be interested not so much in exploring the space but in reaching a point within it quickly, which can happen through directed search (prompting an AI system or making a request to it) or combination of existing elements (combinatorial creativity in Boden's terms). 

The analysis of the next chapter offers further insight into Transformational Creatives, and the relationship between dialogic affordances, roles, and motivations of users. 

\subsection{Chapter 6: LLMs in two generative sonic installations}

Lastly, let us examine the case studies presented in Chapter 6, which were two installations developed for the Australian Cybernetic festival at the Australian National University (System of a Sound), and another for the 50th anniversary of the Sydney Opera House (Music of the Sails). Both installations were iterations of the same core concept: using a Large Language Model to serve as a bridge between incoming data streams, and the control of a generative music engine, with the intention to produce a continuous data-reactive soundscape. 

The core intention was to probe the technology and specifically the practice of data sonification with semantic technologies, such that the resulting soundscape reflect discernable characteristics from the data. The data corresponded to local and global environmental variables, such as energy use, temperature, building occupancy, in the case of the Music of the Sails installation, and variables such as CO2 in the atmosphere, local weather, in the case of the System of a Sound installation. 

What type of dialogic interaction did these systems afford? It is useful to recap how the system works. In a system prompt, we established to the system what the goal of the installation was. We gave it contextual information about what the data meant and how to read it (what column names meant, and so forth). As well as context about how to control the music engine. We created a script that ran the LLM with the system prompt continuously. 

Importantly, we did not have a channel for bidirectional communication, converse or to provide feedback to the model in real time about its performance. We simply set the system prompt and let it run. The inability to communicate in real-time with this LLM to steer its outputs proved a limitation, when we encountered the problem that the system started to fixate on a given musical output consistently. In order to steer it away from that, we could not say ``try something different''. Instead we needed to modify the system prompt, again, transforming the system at a meta-creative level, but not at a performance-time dialogue. 

Given the system was running continuously producing outputs based on our given prompt, we can understand it as well as a pseudo-dialogue, where the system did not model our intentions based on interactions beyond our initial system prompt, and we could not significantly influence it at performance time. 

However, by us being able to define the system prompt and thus the global behavior of the system, we were able to achieve a certain level of mutual influence and understanding. Given we engaged in this process multiple times over the course of the performance based on trial and error,  it could be understood that we also engaged with the system at a meta creative level. 

\subsubsection{How did this influence roles?}

What were our roles, therefore? On one hand, our role can be understood as that of architects or orchestrators. We designed the installations, consisting of multiple tools, and connected them together. Our roles, borrowing a term from generative art pioneer Brian Eno, could also be described as \textbf{gardeners}, a term he has used to describe his role as a generative artist and generative system designer. We planted seeds in the form of system prompts and the generative rules driving the music generation system, which the LLM could choose from, and pruned and modified the system continuously as it grew over multiple days. 

What was the role of the AI? It could be understood as that of a \textbf{translator}, between complex data and music, and that of a continuous \textbf{performer}, continuously engaging in this translation process as part of a performance. In both of these cases, we were interested in exploring the possibility that the AI engaged in roles that no human could. That is, constantly reading a stream of complex numerical data and in a matter of seconds, make a creative decision about how to control a music engine as a result. 

\subsubsection{User Motivations}

Going back to our lens of motivation, we were interested in transforming the space of possibilities for data sonification practice, creating a new type of tool, or system, that could generate soundscapes with a level of semantic understanding both of the data and music produced as a result. Moreover, the installations involved more than establishing a system prompt. They involved building the data ingestion pipeline from multiple sources, including weather, internet feeds and power usage of the building. On the other hand, it involved building the generative music engine, which consisted mostly of recorded sounds by the composers within the team. These were stored in a library that could be combinatorially mixed to produce almost infinite variations, with parameters that allowed the engine to control music aspects such as valence, energy, tempo, key and so forth. The AI therefore, was embedded within a larger context of tools and data streams, designed by us with the intention of exploring new creative spaces.

\section{Design Principles}

With this, we arrive now at the Design Principles for Dialogic Co-Creative AI. 

In Chapter 3, I established a framework including a typology of actions a DCAI could take, three levels of dialogic interaction, a set of preliminary design principles. These principles were drawn from a multidisciplinary literature on dialogue, including human-computer interaction, artificial intelligence, philosophy and sociology. These principles provide guidance for systems that can engage in full dialogue, as opposed to pseudo-dialogue or weak-asymmetric dialogue. Importantly, the fundamental intention of these principles is creating co-creative systems that are human-centred, respecting the agency of the user, supporting their creativity, while at the same time, leveraging the particular type of creativity of generative technology discussed in Chapters 1 and 2. 

In this section, I revisit these principles based on the learnings throughout Chapters 4, 5 and 6, and based on an updated literature review that considers the literature and practice that emerged since the beginning of this thesis. With rapid progress both in capabilities and adoption, many insights have emerged in parallel to my research, and these principles form an effort to integrate these with my own research. 

Six principles are presented, rephrased from the initial principles presented in Chapter 3. While Chapter 3 presented them as general principles for dialogic co-creativity, here they are framed as specific design principles and guidelines for Dialogic Co-Creative Artificial Intelligence.

\subsection{Design for Roles and Motivations}

Before applying the six design principles outlined above, designers must first consider the user: who they are, what motivates them, and what role they wish to play in the creative process. This meta-principle serves as a starting point for prioritising and adapting the other principles to specific contexts.

Two key questions guide this process. First, what are the motivations of the user? As discussed in Chapter 2, users of co-creative systems may be Transactional Creators, seeking efficient outputs with minimal involvement; Casual Creators, engaging for enjoyment rather than outcome; or Transformational Creators, seeking to push creative boundaries. Each type of user will value different aspects of the interaction, influencing how the Dialogic Co-Creative AI system is designed. 

Second, what roles will the user assume when interacting with the system? Users may shift between roles such as director, collaborator and curator, or audience depending on the task and their goals. A system designed for users wishing to act as directors will differ from a user seeking to collaborate, or improve their own skills with feedback from the AI. 

By beginning with these questions, designers can guide decisions about which principles to emphasise and how to specifically implement them. It is important to note that mapping specifically how each design principle can be weighted and implemented for each type of user remains an area for further research. As such, this principle is not prescriptive but rather acts as an invitation to use questions as a starting point. 


\subsection{Design for Communication}

This research project began in 2021, before the widespread adoption of chatbots such as ChatGPT, which launched in November 2022. One of my initial proposals for dialogic interaction was to investigate both the capabilities and impact of having generative systems communicate with users through conversation. In Chapter 3, I presented a research paper at the CHI workshop on Generative AI and HCI that explored the capability of an early Large Language Model to engage in dialogic interaction. At the time, LLMs functioned exclusively as text completion systems rather than conversational agents: a string was passed as a prompt and the next word or words were generated. However, emerging approaches, including my own, began demonstrating that this capability could be exploited to produce conversational interaction if the initial prompt was framed as a series of pre-existing messages in a conversation. In that paper, I showed that the system had the emergent capability of switching between discussing the writing and producing the writing itself, and I identified a series of possible actions that human and AI could engage in. This type of interaction proved more conducive to collaboration, enabling human and AI to discuss creative goals while also directly contributing to the creative output. In November 2022, ChatGPT was launched, similarly repurposing a linear language model to engage in conversational interaction. Its rapid adoption illustrated the potential of this approach.

A starting point for Dialogic Co-Creative AI is therefore to offer a space for user and AI to communicate conversationally. The widespread adoption of chat interfaces for generative AI systems has demonstrated both the potential and the limitations of this mode of interaction. Text-based communication, while accessible, can constrain creative exchange. For example, a study by \citet{Park2024-gw} titled ``We are visual thinkers, not verbal thinkers!'' analysed how designers use generative AI tools and found that one of their main struggles was conveying visual intentions through text.

Multimodal inputs offer promise for more expressive interaction, allowing users to communicate creative intentions more richly. In Chapter 5, I described how we leveraged image references to achieve more granular control of outputs, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:ai_generation_workflow}. I also discussed the importance of training the model on a set of images, thereby communicating creative preferences implicitly rather than through explicit verbal description.

In a study by \citet{Peng2024-tr}, the authors described an interface that allows designers to curate moodboards consisting of colour palettes, images, and text, and to use these to drive generation. This interface, they found, allowed designers to ``explore and express themselves more effectively'' than text-to-image tools alone.

Such interfaces enable richer communication of creative intention, allowing users to show what they mean when words fall short.

\subsection{Design for Collaboration}

In Chapter 4, I demonstrated the value of separating spaces for communication from spaces for collaboration by providing a dedicated space where human and AI could work together directly. This separation increased user involvement and perceptions of agency. Consider, for example, the following quote from a participant using the chat-only interface, without a dedicated space for collaboration:

\begin{quote}
``[It] made me feel like I was cheating somehow. It does not feel like my work, even though I gave all the ideas. Also, I believe there is satisfaction in putting a lot of effort/dedication/patience into something. Vorges [the AI] made everything so simple, fast, and easy that it felt artificial and no real satisfaction came as a result.''
\end{quote}

In contrast, a prototype enabling a shared editor, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:shared-editor}, allowed users to collaborate more closely at the level of the creative writing itself, beyond communicating only at the level of instructions and ideas.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{sharededitor.png}
    \caption{The shared editor prototype, combining a chat interface with a direct manipulation space.}
    \label{fig:shared-editor}
\end{figure}

Participants using the shared editor reported markedly different experiences:

\begin{quote}
``I really-really enjoyed writing this. I even had a deep moment of reflection, my writing was nostalgic and sad, but I was able to use AI to steer it in the right direction, it gave me confidence that I was also writing with correct grammar and spelling, English is not my first language and while I am proficient, I can still use proofreading to ensure good quality, this tool helped me with it.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
``I liked how my original ideas were still retained, and AI was used to complement my intentions. It forced me to put in some effort and do the majority of the work.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
``It adds an element of working together, which I think is the moral problem with current AI tools—they often seem like they're doing all the work.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
``It was much better than ChatGPT. I enjoyed how it gave me a lot more agency.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
``I really enjoyed it; it still let me have autonomy.''
\end{quote}

Across these responses, a consistent theme emerged: the shared editor preserved user autonomy and fostered a sense of genuine collaboration rather than delegation.

However, the research also revealed that designing a space for collaboration is not trivial. A recurring concern was the need for improved visibility of the AI's contributions:

\begin{quote}
``When you ask the AI to check your grammar (as I did), it would be good if it told me what suggestions it had made, so I can double check its work easier.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
``I am unsure as what parts of the text are being edited, in the end I am not quite sure which parts were mine and which ones were edited by AI.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
``Once the tool has made revisions to the original text, maybe it can highlight the key changes that have been made... otherwise I need to slowly read through and identify the changes myself.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
``[...] in an AI chatbox, usually you would parse through the original text copy into the chat, and the chat would then spit out a new copy, makes it a bit easier to edit/cut out any additions you don't like.''
\end{quote}

These findings align with those of \citet{Buschek2021-ks}, who discuss ``conflicts of territory'' as one of nine potential pitfalls when designing human-AI co-creative systems. They describe how AI may override user contributions and make destructive changes, and suggest mitigations including highlighting AI-introduced changes, allowing revisions, and providing version control. These recommendations align closely with the suggestions made by participants in my own study.

Designing for collaboration, therefore, requires not only providing a shared space but also ensuring that contributions within that space remain visible, attributable, and reversible. Without such safeguards, the benefits of collaboration risk being undermined by confusion over authorship and loss of user control.

\subsection{Design for Mutual Influence}

As discussed in Chapters 2 and 3, mutual influence is a core component of dialogic co-creativity. Rather than a unidirectional flow where the user simply directs the system, dialogic co-creativity involves both parties shaping and being shaped by the creative process. This reciprocal dynamic suggests that the AI's contributions can valuably redirect the creative trajectory towards new, unanticipated directions.

In Chapter 3, I examined the work of \citet{Weisz2024-io} regarding design principles for generative AI systems. They argue that a key design principle involves managing ``generative variability,'' which is the tendency for tools to move in divergent directions. While they propose guidelines to manage this variability, their strategies primarily focus on \textit{mitigation}. In the context of co-creativity, however, generative variability may be considered a functional feature rather than a flaw. This perspective is reflected in a comment from a participant in my co-creative writing studies:

\begin{quote}
``Vorges is like a creative collaborator or editor with ADHD - not always on point and occasionally disordered, but with no shortage of ideas.''
\end{quote}

In creativity studies, surprise and serendipity are often recognised as crucial for the creative process \citep{Simon1967-nr, Lubart2001-vl, Grace2015-uc, Moruzzi2022-gp}. Multiple studies suggest that the introduction of new perspectives and novelty may be among the most valued features of generative AI for users engaged in creative activities \citep{Lawton2023-tb, Park2024-gw, Koch2020-gx}. Similar views are echoed by creative practitioners, such as art director Lucas Crespo:

\begin{quote}
Creative work isn't only about precision. It's about discovery, exploration, and happy accidents that lead to breakthroughs. The more you can induce the feeling of serendipity in a creative tool, the more it keeps me prompting and pushing it to its limits.

As AI advances, I worry we'll optimize too heavily for accuracy at the expense of serendipity. We'll build tools that give us exactly what we ask for, rather than what we didn't know we needed.

The best creative tools should feel like collaborators, not calculators. They should surprise, challenge, and push us in new directions. Working with them should feel more like jamming with a slightly unpredictable bandmate. It's the difference between following a map and exploring without one. \citep{Crespo2025-wr}
\end{quote}

Mutual influence, therefore, is largely about serendipity: helping users find creative possibilities they did not know they were looking for.

How can we design interactions that promote serendipity? One promising approach lies in \textbf{exploration-based interfaces}. \citet{Davis2024-ml} demonstrated that a multimodal interface allowing two-dimensional exploration of the latent space of fashion designs enabled better ideation than prompt-based text-to-image interfaces, such as the Stable Diffusion tool they tested against. They concluded that ``text-only prompts in existing models restrict creative exploration, especially for novices.'' Their interface allowed users to explore the latent space of designs along semantically meaningful directions (such as sleeve length or pattern) and included a style-mixing panel for blending designs. The study found that this interface was more conducive to ideation than the request-execute interactions found in modern text-to-image systems. Similarly, \citet{Koch2020-gx} tested an ideation tool specifically focused on enabling serendipity, demonstrating its value for divergent exploration in human-computer partnerships.

Beyond exploration-based interfaces, another way of enabling mutual influence is by \textbf{leaning into the unique characteristics of the generative AI medium itself}. As Brian Eno puts it:

\begin{quote}
Whatever you now find weird, ugly, uncomfortable and nasty about a new medium will surely become its signature. CD distortion, the jitteriness of digital video, the crap sound of 8-bit - all of these will be cherished and emulated as soon as they can be avoided \citep{Eno2007-fl}.
\end{quote}

Highlighting rather than hiding the weirdness and uniqueness of AI output may be creatively useful. As one participant in my study in Chapter 4 noted, regarding the introduction of divergent concepts in their writing:

\begin{quote}
``I also love its figurative language and descriptions because of how they kinda just don't make sense. I left that in on purpose because I love the idea of a curious nose and a perpetually second-hand jacket.''
\end{quote}

\citet{Sloan2016-fj}, an author describing his process using a generative language model, expressed a similar sentiment: ``The goal is not to make the resulting text `better'; it's to make it different—weirder, with effects maybe not available by other means.'' Similarly, \citet{Samuel2019-gc}, discussing her experience with language models, argues that art often enhances our perception by ``making the familiar strange.'' She posits that AI can be an effective tool for writers precisely because:

\begin{quote}
``it's great at defamiliarizing our world. The human-ish language it generates can startle us into seeing things anew, so we can in turn jolt readers awake through that sense of -ish.''
\end{quote}

In my case study with the AFR magazine (Chapter 5), this was precisely our intention: to use the AI system to create ``impossible photography.'' As the editorial team described \citep{Drummond2023-av}:

\begin{quote}
``They are both uncanny and yet slightly unreal. All have the distinctive fuzzy texture of AI images, as if they were drawn. Our prompts were very minimal and the output hints at the way AI is learning 21st-century human culture.''
\end{quote}

Designing for mutual influence, therefore, involves embracing rather than suppressing the generative variability of AI systems. This can be achieved through interfaces that encourage exploration and serendipitous discovery, and through a willingness to foreground the distinctive qualities of AI-generated outputs. The goal is therefore to balance control and convergence with novelty and divergence.

\subsection{Design for Mutual Understanding}

In Chapter 3, I argued that a central element for Dialogic Co-Creative AI is mutual understanding, whereby the human and the AI form mental models of one another. Multiple mechanisms can facilitate this process; this section discusses two specific avenues identified through my research: visibility of affordances and personalisation through training.

The literature review in Chapter 2 identified how providing visibility of capabilities contributes to system explainability, ultimately enabling more effective human-AI interaction. This emerged as a recurring theme in the Chapter 4 co-writing studies, where participants frequently suggested that system improvements should focus on providing greater visibility of and access to system capabilities. This involves understanding what the system can and cannot achieve and leveraging it appropriately. Specific suggestions included the use of drop-down menus with predefined options, pre-written prompts, and the packaging of specific actions through graphical user interface (GUI) elements.

An effective way to help users form an accurate mental model of an AI system is therefore to provide clear visibility of the system's generative affordances. This can be achieved through interface design and GUI elements, or by the system communicating its own capabilities and limitations through natural language. For example, rather than presenting a blank text field that implies unlimited possibility, an interface might offer a set of clearly labelled actions (such as ``expand,'' ``summarise,'' or ``suggest alternatives'') that signal what the system is designed to do well.

Current interfaces for generative systems, however, are converging towards open-ended conversational interactions. These can create the impression that ``anything is possible,'' which may lead to user frustration when the system fails to meet expectations. Such interfaces are at odds with the observed and reported needs for clear visibility of affordances and with established design principles regarding visibility \citep{Nielsen1994-df}.

A complementary mechanism for fostering mutual understanding is personalisation: allowing users to train models on their own datasets. This enables the system to model user preferences while simultaneously allowing the user to understand system behaviour, as the training set is a crucial determinant of the output. Such a process was observed in the Chapter 5 case study, where iterative training enabled more precise control over outputs and a clearer understanding of how text prompt inputs were mapped to results. As the creative team noted:

\begin{quote}
``The fact that you can train Stable Diffusion, by limiting or controlling the inputs it draws upon, makes it better [at consistently generating what we intended]'' \citep{Drummond2023-av}.
\end{quote}

Through this process, the user develops a more accurate mental model of the system by observing how changes to the training data affect outputs, while the system develops a more accurate model of the user's preferences through the curated dataset.

Two strategies for designing for mutual understanding, therefore, involve making the system's capabilities legible to the user and enabling the system to learn the user's preferences through training and personalisation. 

\subsection{Design for Iteration}

Iteration is a crucial aspect of the creative process, and the iterative formation of mutual understanding and a creative product is central to dialogic co-creativity. A Dialogic Co-Creativity AI therefore needs to support collaboration with the user throughout this iterative process, refining outputs towards a final result. However, current generative systems offer limited support for this.

As an illustrative example, consider Figure \ref{fig:albo_series}, from the case study in Chapter 5. We were satisfied with one of the initial outputs, shown on the left, but wished to make a minor modification: for the subject to be smiling. Despite this seemingly small change, which involved a single word addition to the prompt, the resulting image changed significantly. While the general scene and subject's position were retained, a suit jacket was unexpectedly added, and the overall contrast of the image shifted.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{alboexperiments.png}
\caption{Screen-grab from my experiments log from the AFR case study, fixing some parameters while varying others. This example illustrates the difficulty of iterating with generative models. The intention was to change the facial expression of the subject, adding a single word to the prompt while using ControlNets to condition the generation. However, clothing was added, and the brightness and contrast of the image changed as well.}
\label{fig:albo_series}
\end{figure}
This lack of support for the iterative nature of creative processes is often cited by professional creatives as a core limitation of generative AI tools \citep{Palani2024-on}.
In their ``Design Principles for Tools to Support Creative Thinking'', \citet{Resnick2005-fs} emphasise the need for rich history-keeping. Similarly, in their Design Principles for Generative AI Applications, \citet{Weisz2024-io} propose visualising the user's journey, supporting curation and annotation, and highlighting differences or variations across outputs to facilitate iteration.

What might such interfaces look like? Consider \citeauthor{thesephist-2022-xu}'s experimental interface for language generation \citep{thesephist-2022-xu}, which allows the user to visualise alternative paths by modifying and weighting attributes in the generated text.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{linuslee.png}
\caption{Lee's experimental prototype of an interface that allows user to change and weight attributes to generate variations, from \citet{thesephist-2022-xu}}
\label{fig:linusleebranching}
\end{figure}
Contrast this with the linear and sequential interface afforded by the prevalent mode of interaction with LLM-powered chatbots. These interfaces enable sequential iteration, but it can be difficult to revisit and explore a different direction if needed (a principle recommended by \citet{Resnick2005-fs}). When describing their interactions with ChatGPT for writing, participants from the user studies in Chapter 4 provide useful illustrations. One participant expressed:
\begin{quote}
``I've felt frustrated because it's gone in the wrong direction and then requires a lot of input from me to put it on the track that I have in my head.''
\end{quote}
Another simply noted that the process ``feels somewhat random and not as iterative'', while another elaborated:
\begin{quote}
``ChatGPT will always rewrite the entire passage to change just one paragraph, and it is harder to work on one text as input as I often need to scroll back up to see it or continually copy and paste it.''
\end{quote}

Beyond branching interfaces that maintain rich histories, an alternative approach for more iterative co-creation is interfaces that provide a stateful space for collaboration, separate from the conversational space. In such a space, users and AI can collaboratively edit and return to previous versions, as implemented in Chapter 4. Users can refine outputs from the AI and vice versa, though appropriate version control and visibility of contributions remain important considerations.

\subsection{Design for Context}

In Chapter 3, I discussed how dialogue happens within context and argued that context-awareness is a defining characteristic of Dialogic Co-Creativity. From this follows a key design principle: designing for context.

As generative AI systems have become more capable and pervasive, their usefulness increasingly depends on their ability to operate within the relevant context. This means not only reading context but also acting upon it. Generative systems such as language models have evolved from isolated predictive systems to the currently growing agentic paradigm, where systems can sense their surroundings and use tools within that environment. My two installations described in Chapter 6 explored this paradigm in the context of new media creative production, probing what new creative possibilities emerged when a language model could read contextual data from the surrounding environment and act upon it by controlling tools such as the generative music engine.

At the time of developing these installations, the capabilities of these systems to act within context were limited, or at least were not afforded natively. Building them involved ``hacking'' together different approaches to feed context into the models and allow them to control tools through structured outputs. Today, many more approaches exist. Standards such as the Model Context Protocol (MCP) \citep{Anthropic2025-tg}now allow language models to engage with external data sources and control tools in a more systematic way.

These developments have enabled systems to become embedded in users' workspaces in a more integrated manner. Tools such as Cursor \citep{Cursor2024-ow}, which has become one of the most successful co-creative systems for coding, owe much of their success to their ability to integrate into the user's codebase context. This allows users to avoid context switching and instead make requests and collaborate within a shared environment. Cursor's developers have described innovations at the level of context integration, particularly the ability to ingest large amounts of codebase context efficiently. Other tools such as Claude Code and Claude Cowork similarly offer, as their key value proposition, productive integration into the user's local context \citep{Anthropic2025-lr}.

Designing tools that can engage with wider context is also valuable because it allows users to connect them with other tools. As existing research has shown, users increasingly assume the role of orchestrators, connecting multiple tools together to accomplish complex tasks \citep{Palani2024-on}. Emerging approaches such as node-based workflow interfaces allow users to visually connect different generative models and non-generative tools to build more sophisticated pipelines. This pattern was evident in my installations in Chapter 6, where our creative practice became the meta-creative act of connecting multiple tools.

When designing a dialogic co-creative AI system, it is therefore important to consider how the system can connect to other tools in the user's workflow, how it can access local context such as files and previous work, and how it can draw on broader context from the internet or other sources.


\section{Closing Remarks}

This thesis has examined how to design generative AI systems that act as effective co-creators, maintaining human agency while leveraging the creative potential of this technology. Through the development of a framework for Dialogic Co-Creative AI and the analysis of multiple case studies, I have identified key interaction design elements that enable dialogic co-creativity and examined how these elements influence user roles and agency.

The framework presented in Chapter 3, consisting of a typology of actions and elements of dialogic co-creativity, provided a lens through which to analyse diverse co-creative systems. This analysis revealed that the level and type of dialogue afforded by a system influences the roles users assume. Systems that offer interaction only at the language level tend to encourage roles of higher-level orchestration, while systems that provide shared spaces for collaboration at the artefact level enable more involved creative partnerships.

A further finding emerged from this analysis: user motivations play a crucial role in shaping interaction dynamics. The distinction between Transactional, Casual, and Transformational Creatives suggests that different users may require different dialogic affordances. This observation points to the need for more nuanced design approaches that consider not only the technical capabilities of systems but also the diverse motivations and goals of their users.

The six design principles presented here, informed by both theoretical foundations and empirical observations, offer guidance for designing effective Dialogic Co-Creative AI systems. However, these principles and their guidelines are not exhaustive, and their implementation will necessarily vary across different creative domains and use cases. The rapid pace of development in generative AI means that new capabilities and interaction patterns continue to emerge, requiring ongoing critical examination and refinement of these principles and guidelines.

Several limitations of this work should be acknowledged. The case studies examined here represent specific contexts and may not generalise to all creative domains. The user studies in Chapter 4, while revealing, involved incentivised tasks that may not fully capture the dynamics of intrinsically motivated creative work. The professional case studies in Chapters 5 and 6, while offering valuable insights from practice, represent particular creative contexts that may not reflect broader patterns of use.

Future research should explore how these principles apply across different creative domains, what specific interaction design patterns and strategies could be implemented to enable these principles, and investigate how systems might better adapt to different user motivations and roles. As generative AI continues to evolve, the relationship between human and artificial creativity will require ongoing critical engagement, balancing the potential for enhanced creative expression with concerns about agency, authorship, and the nature of creative work itself.

\section{Statement on the Use of AI}
Throughout this thesis, I used Artificial Intelligence, and specifically Large Language Models, both as an object of study and as what I consider to be an emerging form of research partner.

The primary form in which I used it was as a proofreader and copy editor. All of the ideas here are mine, and every section was originally written by me. No text was generated completely using a Large Language Model. Given the aim of my thesis, it was extremely important for me to maintain ownership, involvement and agency. I was, however, interested in exploring the possibility of using it to augment rather than automate my own intellectual work. Some of these explorations failed, but illuminated important limitations of Artificial Intelligence as a creative partner.

It also illuminated possibilities. For example, for literature reviews, I collected hundreds of papers and developed a pipeline that ingested each PDF to generate a summary of key ideas related to my research questions, as individual files in markdown format. I then ran another script that used an LLM to find connections between ideas. Using a canvas node-interface in Obsidian, I visualised the connected ideas and used it to gain a high-level conceptual understanding of the research landscape. I believe this type of cognitive tool is genuinely new and enables novel possibilities in intellectual and creative work.

I also used it to validate consistency of concepts across this long document, using Cursor, an AI-enabled co-programming environment traditionally used for software development. However, I used it instead to carefully inspect my LaTeX files that comprise the chapters, at scale, finding terminology and conceptual inconsistencies. 

Importantly, as stated in this Introduction, Large Language Models and AI changed drastically from the onset of this research in 2021 until today, in 2025, evolving from very rudimentary short text generators to integrated agents in workspace environments. This evolution has shaped my thinking about the possibilities, dangers and limitations of this technology. As discussed in the introduction, I now believe it can genuinely be a tool that expands human cognition and creativity in genuinely important ways. I also believe that automating these processes, or outsourcing them, can be detrimental for the product, the individual, and collective knowledge and creative production. It is my hope that this thesis helps provide valuable ideas, a framework and principles for designing AI that enables the first possibility.
