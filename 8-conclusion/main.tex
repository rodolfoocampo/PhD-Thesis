\section{Further analysis: relationship between dialogic affordances, roles and type of interaction}

Let's look closer at the elements of dialogic co-creativity present in each co-creative system and their relationship to roles. 

\subsection{Chapter 4: two types of writing AI co-creators}

Let’s begin by considering the comparative user studies performed in Chapter 4. Two types of co-creative writing systems were compared across user studies in a creative writing task, one offered interaction with the AI only through a chat interface while the other through a chat and a shared text editor between the human and the AI. 

From the perspective of the DCAI framework, the chat-only interface primarily offered interactions \textit{about} the artefact. The user and the AI could discuss goals, feedback and clarificiations through the chat window. However, there was no space for both of them to collaborate together on the writing. On the other hand, the the chat-and-editor was developed to enable this shared space of interaction allowing for interaction and communication about the writing, but also interaction directly \textit{through} the writing. 

We can further classify these types of co-creative system based on their level of dialogue shown in Figure \ref{fig:levelsofdialogue}. The interaction between the human and AI in the chat-only interface stayed at the level of language, while in the chat-and-editor happened both in the language space and the artefact space. 

As such, we can understand the chat-only co-creative system offering something akin to \textbf{weak-asymmetric dialogue}, while the chat-and-editor offered something closer, but not fully, akin to full dialogue. In both cases, however, mutual modelling, understanding and influence was present but limited: the AI simply did was not designed to actively engage in a process of actively modelling the user. 

\subsubsection{How did this influence roles?}

Users in the chat-only interface adopted a dynamics closer to a client-producer relationship, with the human user assuming roles such as \textbf{director}, \textbf{requester}, \textbf{outsourcer} and \textbf{ideator}, automating most of the writing process. Users in the \textbf{Chat-and-Editor} reported being more involved, and reported contributing more closely to the writing. While they still reported asking the AI to do the heavy-lifting of writing, they \textbf{reported the writing contained their own words more}, and \textbf{described themselves as more involved in the writing process}. 

Consider the following quotes from users of the \textbf{Chat-only interface}:

\begin{quote}
"Vorges wrote 90\% of the story based on my prompts. I just tweaked it a bit." (Participant, Chat-only)

"It did everything—I just gave it the topic." (Participant 6, Chat-only)

"I gave it the idea, and it just took it from there, writing almost everything." (Participant 7, Chat-only)

"I gave it the skeleton of the story, and Vorges fleshed it out—almost like giving the recipe and having it cook the dish." (Participant 11, Chat-only)
\end{quote}

On the other hand, consider the following quotes from participants using the \textbf{Chat-and-Editor interface}: 

\begin{quote}
"Vorges gave me ideas, and I picked the ones that worked for me, blending them into my own writing." (Participant 1, Chat-and-Editor)

"I wrote the draft, and Vorges made the language more advanced and descriptive." (Participant, Participant 1, Chat-and-Editor)

"It was like I was the curator of the story—I picked the pieces I liked and left the rest." (Participant 8, Chat-and-Editor)

"It was like a brainstorming partner, throwing out ideas I hadn’t thought of." (Participant 11, Chat-and-Editor)
\end{quote}


These qualitative findings align with the quantitative results shown in Figure \ref{fig:graphlikert}, where users in the Chat-Only interface were more likely to agree with the statement: "Vorges (the AI) did most of the work" than users of the Chat-and-Editor. Conversely, users in the Chat-and-Editor interface were more likely to agree that the final story contained words that they wrote. 

\begin{figure}

    \caption{Comparison of mean scores for the Likert Scale from Chapter 4, highlighting differences about the level of users involvement in the final work in chat-only vs chat-and-editor prototype groups.}
    \label{fig:graphlikert}

It is important to note, however, that while they were differences in level of agreement, \textbf{both groups still reported agreement or strong agreement about the AI doing most of the work}. Moreover, in both cases, the roles described are those of higher level orchestration: providing ideas, curation, direction, while the AI did the work of putting words on the page. These findings align with similar results reported in the literature: in a systematic review of emerging roles and workflows in human-AI in creative practice, Palani et al. \cite{Palani2024-on} found that users increasingly assume roles at the "Project" level while AI assumes roles at the "Artefact" level. My own findings suggest that this dynamic is more pronounced in co-creative systems that offer weak-assymetric dialogue, which allow interaction at the language level, but not at the artifact level through a space for collaboration. This finding is not surprising: systems that do not provide affordances for the user to interact at the artefact level see them engage in less artefact level roles. 

\subsection{User Motivations}

Another possible reason for both groups reporting that the AI did most of the writing may lie in their motivations. In my studies, the users were asked to complete a writing task and were incentivise to do so, providing an extrinsic motivation, which has been shown to produce less involvement in the process compared to intrinsic motivation \cite[15]{Amabile1983-lj}. 

Compton's work on Casual Creativity serves as a useful theoretical lens to further explain this \cite{Compton2015-sy}. Compton argues that a crucial yet often overlooked dimension to understand users is their motivations. She argues that there are least two types of users based on their motivations. On one hand, there are users that are task-oriented and simply want a get the job done. On the other, she proposed the term Casual Creators for an emerging type of user of creative tools. These are users that are process-oriented and engage with the tool for the sake of enjoyment. She argues these two types of users elicit two different types of design spaces: they have different needs and motiviations. I will henceforth will the refer to the former as \textbf{Transactional Creatives}, and the latter as \textbf{Casual Creatives}. 

Given the incentivised nature of my studies, and the assigned creative task, most of the users in the studies in Chapter 4 fall in the \textbf{Transactional Creatives category}, which may explain why they reported\textbf{ that AI did most of the work}. In contrast, a small minority, reported engaging in the experience with the intention of playing with the tool, and reported they generally enjoy creative writing. This small minority of users may be understood more as casual creatives. Interestingly, while not sufficient to make a statistically significant claim, these users happened to Strongly Disagree with the also Strongly the statement "Vorges (the) did most of the work". 

So while the type of dialogic interaction afforded influenced the role assumed by the users, another crucial aspect, which I did not anticipate at the onset of the research and the study design, are \textbf{user motivations}. Transactional creatives may be inclined to be less involved, assuming outsourcer and directive roles, while casual creatives, who in this case enjoy creative writing may be more inclined to be more involved and assumed roles closer to a creative partnership. One may speculate, casual creatives experience may benefit from affording full-dialogue, having the opportunity to contribute to the artefact level through a shared collaborative space, while transactional creatives may be contempt with weak asymmetric dialogue interacting at the level of language, that affords task outsourcing rather than deep involvement. This hypothesis and its potential nuances present a promising area to explore further. 

\subsection{Chapter 5: further analysis of an image-generation co-creative AI professional case study}

In Chapter 5, I described a generative AI image system used the production of visual for a nationally printed magazine and newspaper in Australia in collaboration with their creative team. The intention was to produce photographs for the people they have listed that year as the most powerful in Australia, placing them in impossible situations using AI, to showcase their personality and work in creative ways.

The system used exhibited a prompt-generation functionality. There was not conversational interface. In this system, the user simply types a prompt, and an image is generated. Iteration and refinement as produced artefact is extremely limited. We could not take an image we liked and asked to make targeted changes. Instead, every image was always regenerated from scratch, limiting refinement. The system offered no interface to make manual changes to the image, and to make modificiations at the pixel level, we needed to bring the image into a separate design software.

As such, the system simply afforded limited actions about the artefact (Request) to the user and virtually no actions \textit{through} the creation. 

\subsubsection{How did this influence roles?}

With such a linear prompt and response interaction, it may be assumed that the level of dialogue afforded was that of \textbf{pseudodialogue}, one where only the user is modelling the system, but the system is not modelling or significantly being influenced by the user interaction. Except interestingly, a crucial feature of the system was that it allowed us to train on our own dataset of images. In our case, we wanted to train on the resemblance of the people we were generating and on our desired style.  This can be understand as an affordance not at the dialogic level, but at a meta dialogic level. The system did not model the user's preferences from repeated interaction, instead, by training the system, we could help the system, if implicitely, model our preferences, and similarly, it allowed us to gain a better understanding of the system, contributing at least partly to the principles of mutual influence and mutual understanding. 

In the previous case study, I discussd how linear interaction patterns, request-generation dynamics may contribute to the user assuming less involved roles where they simply outsource creative production. This may have been the case in the image generation case study in chapter 5, however, by providing us with the capability of training the system, our role became that of curators. We the first gathered an initial set of images to train on, we then generated using the system trained on these images, and based on the outputs, went on to collect a different set of images to retrain the system, as well as select some outputs we liked to feedback in the training process. Similarly, throughout the process, we learned to prompt better, understanding what words were mapped to what outputs. Our role, therefore, was that of prompters and curators. We not only curated images to train on, but we produced thousands of images and curated the best ones in a highly involved process that lasted around 1 months. 

Therefore, it appears that when a system affords the ability to train it with datasets, the user assumes the role of curator both of inputs and outputs. 

\subsection{User Motivations}

What can we say about our motivations? Were we acting as Transactional Creatives or as Casual Creatives? The work was done as part of a commercial creativity context, producing visuals for a magazine. However, the motivation of the work itself was not to speed up a process, but rather, the stated intention was to probe the limits of generative AI, and to produce a new type of image that was not possible with traditional photography. Our motivation therefore, was not necessarily to enjoy the process, as a casual creative would, or get the job done quickly, as a transactional creative would, but to probe and push creative boundaries. I propose this type of user can be called Transformational Creative.  A crucial feature for this type of user is, therefore, the ability to meta-interact with the system, being able to mould it, personalise it, train it and even break it. Transforming the tool and their associated possibilities is part of the creative process itself for this type of user. As Boden describes \cite{Boden2003-hk}, transformational creativity happens when the space of possibility is transformed, and this may happen when a new tool if created or an existing one modified. In contrast, a Casual Creative may be more interested simply in traversing an existing space (what Boden calls exploratory creativity), while a Transactional Creatove may be interested not so much in exploring the spade but in reaching a point within it quickly, which can happen through directed search (prompting an AI system or making a request to it) or combination of existing elements (combinatorial creativity in Boden's terms). 

The analysis of the next chapter offers further insight into Transformational Creatives, and the relationship between dialogic affordances, roles, and motivations of users. 

\section{Chapter 6: LLMs in two generative sonic installations}

Lastly, let's look at  the case studies presented in Chapter 6, which were two installations developed for the Australian Cybernetic festival at the Australian National University (System of a Sound), and another for the 50th anniversary of the Sydney Opera House (Music of the Sails). Both installations were iterations of the same core concept: using a Large Language Model to serve as a bridge between incoming data streams, and the control of a generative music engine, with the intention to produce a continuous data-reactive soundscape. 

The core intention was to probe the technology and specifically the practice of data sonificiaiton with semantic technologies, such that the resulting soundscape reflect discernable characteristics from the data. The data corresponded to local and global environmental variables, such as energy use, temperature, building occupancy, in the case of the Music of the Sails installation, and variables such as CO2 in the atmosphere, local weather, in the case of the System of a Sound installation. 

What type of dialogic interaction did these systems afford? It is useful to recap how the system works. In a system prompt, we established to the system what the goal of the installation was. We gave it cotxtual information about what the data meant and how to read it (what column names meant, and so forth). As well as context about how to control the music engine. We created a script that ran the LLM with the system prompt continuously. 

Importantly, we did not have a channel for bidirectional communication, converse or to provide feedback to the model in real time about it's performance. We simply set the system prompt and let it run. The inability to commuicate in real-time with this LLM to steer its outputs proved a limitation, when we encountered the problem that the system started to fixate on a given musical output consistently. In order to steer it away from that, we could not say "try something different". Instead we needed to modify the system prompt, again, transforming the system at a meta-creative level, but not at a performance-time dialogue. 

Given the system was running continuosly producing outputs based on our given prompt, we can understand it as well as a pseudodialogue, where the system did not model our intentions based on interactions beyond our initial system prompt, and we could not significantly influence it at performance time. 

However, by us being able to define the system prompt and thus the global behavior of the system, we were able to achieve a certain level of mutual influence and understanding. Given we engaged in this process multiple times over the course of the performance based on trial and error,  it could be understood that we also engaged with the system at a meta creative level. 

\subsubsection{How did this influence roles?}

What were our roles, therefore? On one hand, our role can be understood as that of architects or orchestrators. We designed the installations, consisting in multiple tools, and connected them together. Our roles, borrowing a term from generative art pioneer Brian Eno, could also be described as \textbf{gardeners}, a term he has used to described his role as a generative artists and generative system designer. We planted seeds in the form of system prompts and the generative rules driving the music generation system, which the LLM could choose from, and pruned and modified the system continuosly as it grew over multiple days. 

What was the role of the AI? It could be understood as that of a \textbf{translator}, between complex data and music, and that of a contnuos \textbf{performer}, continuously engaging in this translation process as part of a performance. In both of these cases, we were interested in exploring the possibility that the Ai engaged in roles that no human could. That is constantly reading a stream of complex numerical data and in a matter of seconds, make a creative decision about how to control a music engine as a result. 

\subsubsection{User Motiviations}

Going back to our lens of motiviation, we were interested in transforming the space of possibilities for data sonificiation practice, creating a new type of tool, or system, that could generate soundscapes with a level of semantic understanding both of the data and music produced as a result. Moreover, the installations involved more than establishing a system prompt. They involved building the data ingestion pipeline from multiple sources, including weather, internet feeds and power usage of the building. On the other hand, it involved building the generative music engine, which consisted on mostly recorded sounds by the composers within the team. These were stored in a library that could be combinatorially mixed to produce almost infinite variatinons, with parameters that allowed the engine to control music aspects such as valence, energy, tempo, key and so forth. The AI therefore, was embedded within a larger context of tools and data streams, designed by us with the intention of exploring new creative spaces.

\section{Design Principles}

With this, we arrive now at the Design Principles for Dialogic Co-Creative AI. 

In Chapter 3, I established a framework including a typology of actions a DCAI could take, three levels of dialogic interaction, a set of preliminary design principles. These principles were drawn from a multidisciplinary literature on dialogue, including human-comuter interaction artificial intelligence, philosophy and sociology. These principles provide guidance for sytems that can engage in full-dialogic interaction, as opposed to pseudo-dialogue or weak assymetric dialogue. Importantly, the fundamental intention of these principles is creating co-creative systems that are human-centred, respectin the agency of the user, supporting their creativity, while at the same time, leverging the particular type creativity of generative technology discussed in Chapters 1 and 2. 

In this section, I revisit these principles based on the learnings throughout Chapters 4, 5 and 6, and based on an updated literature review that considers the literature and practice that emerged since the beggining of these thesis. With rapid progress both in capabilities and adoption, many insights have emerged in paralell to my research, and these principles form an effort to integrate these with my own research. 

Six principles are presented, rephrased from the initial preliminary design principles presented in Chapter as follows:

\subsection{Design for Communication}

The current research project began in 2021, before the pervasive adoption of chatbots such as ChatGPT launched in November 2022. As such, one of our initial proposals of dialogic interaction was investigating both the capabilities and impact of having generative systems communicate with the user in conversational interaction. In Chapter 3, my first research paper presented at the CHI workshop on Generative AI and HCI, precisely explored the capability of an early Large Language Model to engage in dialogic interaction. LLMs at the time functioned exclusively as text completion rather than conversational agents: a string was passed (the prompt) and the next word or words were generated. However, emerging "hacky" approaches such as ours began demostrating theis capability could be exploited to produce conversational interaction if the initial prompt was framed as the a series of prexisting messages in a conversation. In our paper we showed that the system had the emergent capability of switching between discussing the writing, and doing the writing itself, and we identified a series of possible actions that human and AI could engage in. We showed this type of interaction was more condusive to a collaborative interaction, where human and AI could discuss the creative goals but also directly contribute to the creative goal. Later on, in November 2022, ChatGPT was launched, similarly repurpoising a linear language model to engage in conversational interaction. The rapid adoption of the technology served as an illustration of the potential of this type of interaction. 

As such, a starting point for a Dialogic Co-Creative AI is simply offering a space for the user and AI to commuicate conversationally. The wide adoption of chat-interfaces with generative Ai systems has demosntated the potential of this type of interaction but also its limitiations. Reducing creative interactions to language interaction limits can be limiting. For example, a study by Park et al. \cite{Park2024-gw} titled "We are visual thinkers, not verbal thinkers!" analysing how designers use generative AI tools found that one of their main struggles was to convey visual intentions through text. 

Multimodal inputs offer promise for more expressive interaction, allowing the user to more richly communicate creative intentions. For example, in Chapter 5, I described how we leveraged image references to drive more granular control of the outputs, as shown in Figure  \ref{fig:ai_generation_workflow}, and previously I discussed the importance of being able to train the model on a set of images, thus communicating our creative preference implicitly.

I discussed how this increased involvement and perceptions of agency. Consider, for example, the following quote from a participant using the \textbf{chat-only interface}, without a dedicated space for collaboration:
\begin{quote}
"[it] made me feel like I was cheating somehow. It does not feel like my work, even though I gave all the ideas. Also, I believe there is satisfaction in putting a lot of effort/dedication/patience into something. Vorges (the AI) made everything so simple, fast, and easy that it felt artificial and no real satisfaction came as a result."
\end{quote}

 "I liked how my original ideas were still retained, and AI was used to complement my intentions. It forced me to put in some effort and do the majority of the work."

\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
 "When you ask the AI to check your grammar (as I did), it would be good if it told me what suggestions it had made, so I can double check its work easier."
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
    "I am unsure as what parts of the text are being edited, in the end I am not quite sure which parts were mine and which ones were edited by AI."
\end{quote}

"Once the tool has made revisions to the original text, maybe it can highlight the key changes that have been made... otherwise I need to slowly read through and identify the changes myself."

\subsection{Design for Mutual Influence}


In Chapter 3, I discussed the work by Weisz et al \cite{Weisz2024-io} on principles for the design of generative AI systems. They argue that an important design principles is managing generative variability, the tendency for tools to go on divergent directions, and they propose a set of guidelines to do so. While useful, these strategies focus on \textit{mitigation}. However, in the case of co-creativity, generative variability may be a feature rather than a bug.

Consider for example, one comment of a participant of my co-creative writing studies: 

\begin{quote}
"Vorges is like a creative collaborator or editor with ADHD - not always on point and occasionally disordered, but with no shortage of ideas."
\end{quote}

In creativity studies, surprise and serendipity are often recognised as crucial for creativity \cite{Simon1967-nr, Lubart2001-vl, Grace2015-uc, Moruzzi2022-gp} and multiple studies suggest that the introduction of new perspectives, and novely may be one of the most appreciated features of generative AI by users in creative activities \cite{Lawton2023-tb, Park2024-gw, Koch2020-gx}, while similar perspectives are echoes by creative practitioners, such as that of creative lead and art director Lucas Crespo: 

\begin{quote}
Creative work isn't only about precision. It's about discovery, exploration, and happy accidents that lead to breakthroughs. The more you can induce the feeling of serendipity in a creative tool, the more it keeps me prompting and pushing it to its limits.

As AI advances, I worry we'll optimize too heavily for accuracy at the expense of serendipity. We'll build tools that give us exactly what we ask for, rather than what we didn't know we needed.

The best creative tools should feel like collaborators, not calculators. They should surprise, challenge, and push us in new directions. Working with them should feel more like jamming with a slightly unpredictable bandmate. It's the difference between following a map and exploring without one. \cite{Crespo2025-wr}
\end{quote} 

Mutual Influence as framed in the context of Dialogic Co-Creative AI is largely about both the user influenced the creative process and influencing each other. A key way of doing this may be the AI leading the user to directions they did not anticipate, or provide outputs they did not know they were looking for, the definition of serendipity. 

How can we design interactions that promote serendipity? One promising alternative lies in \textbf{exploration-based interfaces}. For instance, a study by \cite{Davis2024-ml} demonstrated that a multimodal interface allowing 2-D exploration of the latent space of fashion designs enabled better ideation than prompt-based text-to-image interfaces, such as the Stable Diffusion tool they tested against. They concluded that "text-only prompts in existing models restrict creative exploration, especially for novices." Their interface allowed users to explore the latent-space of designs along semantically meaningful directions (e.g., sleeve length or pattern) and a style-mixing panel for blending designs. The study found that this interface was reported to be more conducive to ideation than the request-execute interactions found in modern text-to-image systems. 

A study by Koch et al. \cite{Koch2020-gx} testing an ideation tool to support human-computer partnerships evaluated an interface that showed a consistent stream of images, related to the task or not. They found this interface was effective at introducing serendipity and perspective shifts in a creative process by providing a space for divergent exploration.

Another way of enabling mutual influence, such that the system influenced the creative process towards a new direction is by \textbf{leaning into the unique characteristics of the generative AI medium itself}. As Brian Eno puts it:

\begin{quote}
Whatever you now find weird, ugly, uncomfortable and nasty about a new medium will surely become its signature. CD distortion, the jitteriness of digital video, the crap sound of 8-bit - all of these will be cherished and emulated as soon as they can be avoided \cite{Eno2007-fl}.
\end{quote}

As such, highlighting rather than hiding the weirdness and uniqueness of the output may be creatively useful. As one participant in my study in Chapter 4 highlighted related to the introduction of divergent concepts in their writing:

\begin{quote}
"I also love its figurative language and descriptions because of how they kinda just don't make sense. I left that in on purpose because I love the idea of a curious nose and a perpetually second-hand jacket."
\end{quote}

Robin Sloan, an author describing his process using a generative language model expressed a similar sentimient \cite{Sloan2016-fj}: "The goal is not to make the resulting text "better"; it's to make it different—weirder, with effects maybe not available by other means." Similarly, Samuel, discussing her experience with language models and citing Du Sautoy \cite{Samuel2019-gc}, argues that art often enhances our perception by "making the familiar strange." She posits that AI can be an incredible tool for writers precisely because:

\begin{quote}
"it's great at defamiliarizing our world. The human-ish language it generates can startle us into seeing things anew, so we can in turn jolt readers awake through that sense of -ish."
\end{quote}

In my case study with the AFR magazine (Chapter 5), this was precisely our intention: to use the AI system to create "impossible photography". As the editorial team described \cite{Drummond2023-av}:

\begin{quote}
"They are both uncanny and yet slightly unreal. All have the distinctive fuzzy texture of AI images, as if they were drawn. Our prompts were very minimal and the output hints at the way AI is learning 21st-century human culture."
\end{quote}

Akin to how photography spurred impressionism to explore what the human eye sees rather than what the lens captures, generative AI can offer a unique lens to reframe what \textit{we are seeing} by \textit{seeing what the algorithm sees}. As such, rather than seeking exact realism, or perfect adherence, the strength of generative AI systems in creative tools may be in leaning into the weirdness of AI outputs, rather than their mitigation. 


\subsection{Design for Mutual Understanding}

A crucial aspect of mutual understanding in Dialogic Co-Creativity AI is the human and AI being able to model each other, in order to understand their preferences goals, and inner workings. There are multiple ways in which this can be enabled, and indeed the fieald of explainable AI is a large research field with many open questions. 

However, I point to a specific set of mechanisms in which this can be enables illustrated by my own research. A crucial one is providing visibility of systems capabilities. 

For exampple, in my Chapter 4 co-writing studies, a recurring theme was users expressing the the need for better visibility of what sthe system could do. They highlighted potential for drop-down menu's with options, pre-written prompts, and triggering of specific actions with GUI elements. Current interfaces with generative systems are converging towards open-ended conversational interactions, however, a crucual limitiation of this may be that this does not provide to the user any information about what the system is capable of doing. Such open-ended interfaces may be at odds with estalished usability principles, which prioritise visibility of affordances \cite{Nielsen1994-df}. 

Another alternative for mostering mutual understanding between user and AI may be by allowing users to personalise and train the generative system their own data. On one hand, this enables the system to implicitely model user preferences, and on the other, it allows the user to understand system behaviour, as the training set is a crucial determinant of it. Such a process was observed in my Chapter 5 case study. Furthermore, such a process of iteratice training enabled us to gradually have more control of the outputs, and understand how inputs, in the form of text prompts, were mapped to these. As the creative team in the Chapter 5 case study noted: "The fact that you can train Stable Diffusion – by limiting or controlling the inputs it draws upon – makes it better [at consistently generating what we intended]" \cite{Drummond2023-av}.


\subsection{Design for Iteration}



A key component of creative processes is iteration.  As an illustrative example, consider Figure \ref{fig:albo_series}, from the case study in Chapter 5. We were satisfied with one of the initial outputs, shown on the left, but wished to make a minor modification: for the subject to be smiling. Despite a seemingly small change which involved a single word addition to the prompt, the resulting image changed significantly. While the general scene and subject's position were retained, a suit jacket was unexpectedly added, and the overall contrast of the image shifted.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{alboexperiments.png}

The intention was to change the facial expression of the subject, adding a single word to the prompt while using ControlNets to condition the generation. Indeed, this lack of support for the iterative nature of creative processes is often cited by professional creatives as a core limitation. It stems partly from generative variability, and the model's inherent capabilities, but it can also be addressed from the perspective of interaction design. In particular, interaction design can help maintain rich histories so that at least practitioners can better form a mental model of how inputs map to outputs. 

In their "Design Principles for Tools to Support Creative Thinking", Resnick et al \cite{Resnick2005-fs} include the need for rich-history keeping. Similarly, in their Design Principles for Generative AI Applications, Weisz et al. \cite{Weisz2024-io} propose guidelines to manage generative variability that can serve as useful strategies to enable more iterative workflows. Specifically: visualising the user's journey, supporting curation and annotation and highlighting differences or variations across outputs.

Emerging communities of practice are already addressing this through the creation of guides to navigate latent spaces, primarily by systematically documenting input-output relationships in controlled experiments \cite{Smith2022-dm} to visualise creative journeys. In my own research, particularly in case studies 5 and 6, I maintained detailed research journals to track how specific prompts related to their respective outputs; Chapter 6 further elaborates on these experiments.

So while there is a clear need for interfaces that help users move through the process in an iterative, journey-keeping manner, interfaces in generative AI applications remain significantly limited in affording this. For example, my journaling practice emerged primarily as a response to the systems' lack of such an interface. In the case of the image generation case study in Chapter 5, the systems I used merely generated galleries of images that make it difficult to track different paths throughout the journey. In the case of my Chapter 6 case studies, I engaged in trial-and-error to understand how different prompts to the language model led to different outputs. Similarly, the interface was limited in affording an iterative and branching exploration.

How could these interfaces look? Consider, for example Lee's experimental interface for language generation \cite{thesephist-2022-xu}, which allows the user to visualise alternative paths based on modifying and weighing attributes in the generated text. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{linuslee.png}
    \caption{Lee's experimental prototype of an interface that allows user to change and weight attributes to generate variations, from \cite{thesephist-2022-xu}}
    \label{fig:linusleebranching}
\end{figure}


Contrast this with the type of linear and sequential interface afforded by the prevalent mode of interaction with LLM-powered chatbots. These interfaces enable sequential iteration, but it may be difficult to go back and explore a different direction if needed (a recommended principle by Resnick et al \cite{Resnick2005-fs}).  When describing their interactions with ChatGPT to write, participants from the user studies in Chapter 4 provide useful illustrations. One participant expressed:

\begin{quote}
"I've felt frustrated because it's gone in the wrong direction and then requires a lot of input from me to put it on the track that I have in my head."
\end{quote}

Another simply notes that the process "feels somewhat random and not as iterative", while another elaborated:

\begin{quote}
"ChatGPT will always rewrite the entire passage to change just one paragraph, and it is harder to work on one text as input as I often need to scroll back up to see it or continually copy and paste it."
\end{quote}

Beyond branching interfaces that maintain rich histories, an alternative for more iterative co-creation is interfaces that provide a stateful space for collaboration, separate from the conversational space. In such a space, users and AI can collaboratively edit, go back to previous versions. Users can refine outputs from the AI and vice-versa. In the next section, I discuss the advantages and challenges of such an interface, which I developed and explored as part of a prototype tested in Chapter 4. 

\subsection{Design for Context}

In Chapter 3, I discussed how dialogue happens within context, and argued that considering context-awareness was an important characteristic of designing Dialogic Co-Creative Artificial Intelligence. As generative AI systems have become more capable, and pervasive, their ability to be useful for the user largely hinges omn their ability to act within the relevant context. This not only means reading context, but also acting on the world. Generative systems such as language models have gone from isolated predictive systems to the currently growing agentic paradigm, where systems can sense their context and act on tools within that context.  My two installations described in Chapter 6 were an attempt at exploring this paradigm in the context of new media creative production, probing what new creative possibilities were afforded when a language model was able to read its context, in this case in the form of data from the surroudning environment, and act on its context, by controlling a tools such as the generative music engine. 

At the time of the development of these installations, the capabilities of these systems to act within context was limited, or at least was not afforded natively, such that it involved hacking together different approaches to feed context into them and allow them to control tools by asking to output a structured output. Today, many more approaches exist. Tools such as MCP have provided standards for language models to engage with external data sources and control tools. 

This has enabled systems to become embedded in users workspaces in a more itegrated manner. It can be argued that tools such as Cursor, which have become one of the most succesful co-creative systems, in the case of coding, largely owe their success to their ability to integrate into the users code base context, allowing users to not context switch, and instead make requests and collaborate in a shared context. Cursor itself has described innovations at the level of context integrating, being able to ingest lagre amounts of code base context efficiently. Others tools such as Claude Code and Claude Cowork offer, as the kye value proposition, integrating into the users local context productively. 

Similarly, designing tools that can engage with wider context is useful because it allows users to connect them with other tools. As shown in existing research, users are largely assuming the role of orchestrators, connecting together multiple tools. Emerging approaches such a node-base workflow interfaces allow users to visually connect different generative models and non-generative tools to build more complex tools. An instance of this was observed in my installations in Chapter 6, where our creative practice was the meta-creative act of connecting multiple tools. 

When designing a dialogic co-creative AI system, it is important to consider how the system can connect to other tools in the user workflow, how it can connect to their local context, be it files, previous and existing work, as well as larger context, be it in the internet or other sources.
